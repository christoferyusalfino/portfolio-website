Context

Building a first-party data asset from scratch

The client was a leading B2C beauty and personal-care brand in Indonesia. I managed the account as Senior Digital Planner at Omnicom Media, responsible for a digital media budget of over $2.5M annually.

The strategic goal was not immediate sales. It was to build an owned audience — a first-party database of high-purchase-intent consumers the brand could reach repeatedly without paying media cost every time. Newsletter subscription was the acquisition mechanism.

Challenge

At $40 a lead, the model could never scale

A $40 cost per lead makes a first-party data strategy arithmetically impossible. Reaching even 100,000 subscribers would consume $4M — more than the entire annual digital budget, for one objective.

The number was not a market ceiling; it was a symptom. Budget was spread evenly across audiences with very unequal intent, the subscription funnel leaked traffic the brand had already paid for, and creative was not being tested systematically enough to know which message actually worked.

Strategy

Three levers, compounding

01

Segment audiences by actual intent

Budget had been distributed evenly across groups that converted at wildly different rates. Splitting them apart made the difference visible — and once visible, spend could follow intent instead of following habit. In-market beauty and skincare audiences carried disproportionate weight.

02

Fix the funnel before buying more traffic

Every percentage point recovered in subscription completion rate lowers cost per lead without spending another cent on media. Reducing friction between ad click and completed subscription was the cheapest available gain — and it compounds against every future dollar of spend.

03

Test creative continuously, not occasionally

Structured A/B testing on headline and offer framing ran as a standing process rather than a one-off exercise. Headline copy in particular moved click-through rate materially — on Performance Max the account reached a 2.39% CTR, which pulled cost per lead down mechanically.

Why they compound

These levers multiply rather than add. Better targeting raises click-through rate; better creative raises it again; a cleaner funnel converts more of the resulting traffic. Three moderate improvements stacked in sequence produce a change far larger than any one of them alone — which is how a 97% reduction becomes possible without a single dramatic breakthrough.

Execution

Two years, two different problems

2023 · Phase 1 — Diagnose

Find where the $40 was going

Broke performance down by audience, placement and creative to locate the concentrations of waste, and established a clean measurement baseline so later changes could be attributed rather than guessed at.

2023 · Phase 2 — Restructure

Rebuild around intent segments

Rebuilt campaign structure so each intent segment could be budgeted and measured independently, and introduced Google Performance Max to reach subscribers across display, search and video inventory simultaneously. That channel alone delivered subscribers at $0.45 against a $2.11 benchmark.

2023 · Phase 3 — Compound

Push CPL below $1

Continuous creative testing plus funnel friction removal drove cost per lead under $1 within the year, producing 280,000+ first-party retail leads.

2024 · Phase 4 — Do more with less

Double volume on a smaller budget

With efficiency established, the 2024 objective inverted: hold the gains while the budget shrank. Leads more than doubled to 670,000+ while marketing spend fell 38% — achieved by continuing to concentrate budget on the proven segment-and-creative combinations rather than re-expanding reach.

Why it worked

A $40 lead is a diagnosis, not a price

When a number is that far out of line, it is almost never the market setting the price — it is structural waste that nobody has taken apart yet. The useful instinct is to treat an outlier cost as a question rather than a constraint.

The second lesson is about sequencing. Fixing conversion rate before buying more traffic meant every later dollar landed on a funnel that already worked — which is why 2024 could more than double output on a budget that shrank by more than a third.

FAQ

Common questions

How do you reduce cost per lead by 97%?

Rarely through one change. Here it came from three compounding levers: segmenting audiences so budget stopped being spread evenly across unequal groups, removing friction from the subscription funnel so more already-paid-for traffic converted, and running continuous creative A/B tests so the best message carried most of the spend. The starting point matters too — a $40 cost per lead signals structural waste, not a hard floor, so early gains were large.

Can you increase lead volume while cutting ad spend?

Yes, when starting efficiency is poor. In 2024 this account more than doubled annual leads from 280,000+ to 670,000+ while cutting spend 38% — possible only because cost per lead fell faster than budget did. It does not scale indefinitely: efficiency gains eventually flatten, and further volume then requires more budget.

What is a good cost per lead for newsletter subscriptions?

It depends heavily on market and category. In this Indonesian beauty campaign, Google Performance Max delivered subscribers at $0.45 against a $2.11 platform benchmark, and the account overall drove cost per lead below $1. In the US or Western Europe the same funnel would typically cost materially more, so benchmarks should always be read against the local market.
